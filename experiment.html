Method & Materials

A repeatable classroom protocol for measuring the insertion loss of a panel pressed from agricultural residue, using two ordinary phones.

Aim and hypothesis

Aim

To measure how many decibels of airborne sound a panel made from agricultural waste removes, across the frequency range that matters for speech and traffic noise (125 Hz–4 kHz), and to compare different crop residues on the same scale.

Hypothesis

A porous fibre panel will reduce the received level, and the reduction will be larger at high frequencies than at low ones, because short wavelengths are more easily trapped inside the pores while long wavelengths push the whole panel and pass through it.

Panels under test

Any residue that can be chopped, dried and pressed will work. A typical classroom recipe is 80 % chopped residue to 20 % binder (starch paste, PVA or natural resin), pressed in a wooden mould and dried for two days. Keep every panel the same size and thickness so the comparison is fair.

Agricultural residues used to press the test panels
Residue Source crop Character of the fibre What to expect acoustically
Paddy (rice) straw Rice Long, hollow, springy stems Very light and very porous — good above 1 kHz, weak in the bass
Rice husk Rice milling Hard, granular, silica-rich shells Denser than straw, so it adds useful mass as well as absorption
Wheat straw Wheat Stiff tubular stems Similar to paddy straw; presses into a firmer board
Sugarcane bagasse Sugar mills Short, fluffy, tangled fibre Often the best all-round performer — fine pores, easy to press
Coconut coir Coconut husk Coarse, elastic, water-resistant Springy; keeps working when damp, useful in coastal classrooms
Groundnut shell Peanut Brittle, hollow shells Adds mass; crush coarsely so the air pockets survive
Maize husk / cob Maize Papery leaves, spongy cob core Cob pith is remarkably absorbent for its weight
Always test a control Run the same measurement on a sheet of plywood, cardboard or thermocol of the same thickness. A result only means something next to a control — and showing that a waste panel matches or beats plywood is a much stronger claim than a bare number.

Apparatus

Two devices

Any two phones, tablets or laptops with a modern browser. One plays, one listens. They never need to talk to each other — the meter recognises the tone by ear.

A mounting frame

A cardboard box with a window cut in one face, or two stacked books, so the panel can stand upright and cover the entire path between the phones.

Measuring tape and tape

To fix the distance between source and meter, and to mark both positions on the table so the second run repeats the first exactly.

Recommended geometry

  • Source-to-meter distance: 30–50 cm, fixed.
  • Panel exactly halfway, standing square to the line between the devices.
  • The panel must be wider and taller than the gap — otherwise sound simply walks around the edge and you measure the leak, not the panel.
  • Phone speaker and phone microphone pointing at each other.
  • Away from walls and hard surfaces where possible; a soft cloth under the rig stops sound travelling through the table.

Procedure

Follow it in order. The whole sequence takes about five minutes per panel.

  1. Fix the source level and never touch it again

    On the Sound Source device, set the media volume to a comfortable level — loud enough to be clearly above the room, not loud enough to be uncomfortable — and set the on-screen output level. From this moment until the experiment ends, do not change either one. Every decibel of volume drift becomes a decibel of fake insertion loss.

  2. Record the sample details

    On the Sound Meter, enter the panel name, material and thickness. These travel with the readings into the results table and the CSV export.

  3. Measure the ambient noise

    Ask the room for silence and tap Measure ambient. The meter sweeps its filter across all six bands and stores the noise floor for each. Any later reading that sits less than 10 dB above this floor is flagged automatically, because at that point you are measuring the room rather than the panel.

  4. Baseline run — no panel

    With the space between the devices empty, arm the meter for Baseline and press Start sweep on the source. The source plays 125 Hz, 250 Hz, 500 Hz, 1 kHz, 2 kHz and 4 kHz in turn. The meter identifies each tone, waits for it to settle, averages the steady part and files it under the right band. You should see six rows appear by themselves.

  5. Panel run — same everything, panel inserted

    Stand the panel in the gap. Move nothing else. Switch the meter to With panel and run the identical sweep again.

  6. Read, save and repeat

    Open the Results page. Check the two curves, note the insertion loss in each band, save the panel to the library and export the CSV. Then repeat the whole procedure for the next material and the control.

Honest limitations

Declare these in your report. Knowing why a measurement is imperfect is worth more marks than pretending it is not.

Flanking sound

A real laboratory seals the panel into a wall between two isolated rooms. On a table, sound also travels around the panel, over the top, and through the table itself. Your figure is therefore the insertion loss of the whole rig, and will usually be lower than the panel's true transmission loss. A bigger panel and a soft mat both push the number closer to the truth.

The microphone is not a calibrated meter

Phone microphones roll off in the bass, have their own frequency response, and their absolute dB scale depends on a calibration figure you supply. This matters for the absolute level. It largely cancels in the difference between the two runs — which is exactly why insertion loss is the quantity being reported.

Automatic gain control

The meter explicitly asks the browser to disable gain control, noise suppression and echo cancellation, because all three would quietly "helpfully" flatten the very difference you are trying to measure. Not every browser honours the request — Chrome and Firefox on Android are the most reliable.

Small speakers, weak bass

A phone speaker produces very little energy at 125 Hz. If the baseline reading in that band is barely above the noise floor, the band is flagged and should be excluded from your average. Using a small Bluetooth speaker as the source fixes this — the site behaves exactly the same way.

Hearing safety Test tones are far more tiring than music at the same level. Keep the source at a moderate volume, never hold a playing speaker near anyone's ear, and give the room a break between sweeps. If a tone is uncomfortable, it is too loud — lower it and re-run both runs.

Ready to measure

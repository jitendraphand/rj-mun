Turning farm waste into quiet rooms

How much noise can a panel made from crop waste actually stop?

Open this website on two devices. One becomes the loudspeaker that plays a test tone at a known level. The other becomes the sound-level meter that measures what gets through. Place a panel between them, and the difference between the two readings is the answer — measured in decibels, on the spot.

Two devices, one measurement

Everything runs inside the browser — no app to install, no internet needed once the page has loaded, and no data leaves the device.

Device 1 — the Sound Source

Plays a pure tone at any of the six standard octave bands (125 Hz to 4 kHz), at a level you set and keep fixed. A one-tap automatic sweep steps through every band so both runs of the experiment are identical.

Open the Sound Source

Device 2 — the Sound Meter

Uses the microphone to show a live decibel reading, works out which tone it is hearing, and records the level for that band automatically. Run it once without the panel and once with it — the meter keeps both sets.

Before you start The Sound Meter needs microphone permission, which browsers only grant on a secure connection. Publish this site over https:// (GitHub Pages works) or open it as http://localhost. Opening the file directly from a USB drive will show the meter but the microphone will stay blocked.

Why crop waste makes a good sound absorber

India burns or dumps millions of tonnes of crop residue every year. The same material, chopped and pressed into a board, is a genuinely useful acoustic material — for the same reason it is a nuisance in a field: it is fibrous, light and full of air.

Porosity

Straw, husk and coir panels are a maze of tiny connected air pockets. Sound entering the maze drags air back and forth through narrow channels, and friction turns that motion into a very small amount of heat. The sound energy is spent instead of passed on.

Mass and damping

A panel also has to be heavy enough to resist being pushed by the sound wave. Fibre boards are lighter than concrete, so they lose to a brick wall at low frequencies — but they beat it badly on cost, weight and carbon footprint.

A second life for a waste stream

Paddy straw, wheat straw, sugarcane bagasse, coconut coir, groundnut shell and maize husk are all agricultural residues. Turning them into panels keeps them out of the stubble-burning cycle that fills the winter air with smoke.

What the numbers mean

The meter reports decibels, a logarithmic scale. That has one consequence worth memorising before you present your results:

  • 3 dB quieter — only just noticeable.
  • 10 dB quieter — most people call it half as loud.
  • 20 dB quieter — a quarter as loud; speech behind the panel becomes a mumble.

So a panel scoring 12 dB is not "12 % better". It is cutting the perceived loudness of the noise roughly in half.

Read the full method

The one number you are measuring

Insertion loss

IL = Lwithout panel − Lwith panel

Measure the level at the meter with nothing in the way. Put the panel in the way. Measure again, changing nothing else. The drop in decibels is the insertion loss of that panel at that frequency.

Because it is a difference between two readings from the same microphone, it stays valid even if the phone has never been calibrated against a proper sound-level meter.

Run it in five minutes

  1. Set up the two phones

    Face them towards each other, 30–50 cm apart, on a table. Mark the positions with tape so nothing moves between runs.

  2. Measure the room

    On the Sound Meter, tap Measure ambient in silence. This is the noise floor your panel has to beat.

  3. Baseline sweep

    Arm the meter for Baseline, then start the sweep on the Sound Source with nothing between the phones.

  1. Panel sweep

    Stand the panel between the phones, arm the meter for With panel, and run exactly the same sweep again.

  2. Read the results

    The Results page draws both curves, works out the insertion loss in every band, and exports a CSV for your report.

  3. Compare materials

    Save each panel to the library and put rice husk, bagasse and coir on the same chart.
